# User Research

**Project:** Development of a Platform for Finding Car Repair Specialists with a Rating System Based on Verified Customer Reviews  
**Author:** Dastan  
**Supervisor:** Botagoz Mirzagalikova  
**Related task:** [CRP-2](https://dastan-car-repair.youtrack.cloud/issue/CRP-2)  
**Status:** Six individual driver interviews and preliminary findings documented; supervisor review is pending.

## 1. Purpose

Understand how drivers find repair specialists, assess service quality and use customer reviews. Explore difficulties without assuming that every participant has experienced dishonest repairs. Use the findings to refine the website requirements.

These discovery interviews concern needs and past experiences. They are separate from the later [website evaluation](evaluation-plan.md), which examines usability and implemented behaviour.

Earlier informal conversations provided preliminary context. The response records below are based on material supplied by the project author, who identified the respondents as real drivers. These records are separate from the earlier unrecorded conversations.

## 2. Planned participants and procedure

- The initial target was 3–5 adult drivers with repair or maintenance experience. Six driver response sets have now been supplied. Age eligibility and recruitment details were not supplied.
- Use convenience recruitment and, where possible, include different levels of repair experience. Record this sampling limitation.
- Allow approximately 15–20 minutes per interview, in the participant's preferred language.
- Explain that participation is voluntary, questions may be skipped and the interview may be stopped. Ask permission to take anonymous notes before starting.
- Use participant codes P1, P2 and so on. Do not record names, telephone numbers, number plates, exact addresses or identifying details about repair businesses in the public report.
- Do not record audio or video by default. Obtain separate permission if recording becomes necessary.
- Ask about a concrete recent experience first. Discuss the proposed website only after the experience questions, to reduce prompting.
- Keep any identifiable original notes outside the public repository, with restricted access. Agree a retention period before collecting them. Publish only anonymised summaries; remove details that could indirectly identify someone.

### Suggested introduction

“I am developing a website to help drivers find car repair specialists and read reviews linked to service requests. I would like to understand your experiences of choosing repair services. This interview is voluntary. You may skip any question or stop at any time. I will use anonymous notes for my diploma and will not include your name or identifying details. May I take notes?”

If consent is not given, do not continue or retain answers. This is a proposed introduction, not a record that anyone has consented.

## 3. Interview questions

| ID | Main question | Optional neutral follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | Tell me about the last time you arranged a vehicle repair or maintenance service. | What did you need, and how did you start looking for help? |
| Q2 | How did you find and choose the specialist or garage? | What other options did you consider, and why did you choose this one? |
| Q3 | What information mattered most when making that choice? | Was any important information missing or difficult to understand? |
| Q4 | Did you read or ask for other customers' opinions? How did you use them? | What made an opinion useful or doubtful? If you did not use reviews, why? |
| Q5 | How did you judge whether the service met your expectations? | What could you check yourself, and what remained uncertain? |
| Q6 | What, if anything, was difficult or disappointing during the process? | How did you respond, and what happened afterwards? Positive experiences are also welcome. |
| Q7 | Have you left a review or made a complaint about a repair service? | What encouraged or discouraged you? If not, what would make it worthwhile? |
| Q8 | How would the proposed request-confirmation and review process fit your experience? | What would be useful, confusing or inconvenient? What should happen if the work is unfinished or unsatisfactory? |

Before Q8, give the same neutral description to each participant: “A customer sends a request to a specialist. After the service, the customer confirms whether it took place. A completed, customer-confirmed request allows one review, including a negative review. The website does not independently inspect the repair.” Record reactions as responses to a proposed concept, not evidence of actual website use.

Avoid leading questions such as “Do mechanics usually deceive customers?” Do not suggest an answer or treat an allegation as independently established fact. Ask for examples without requesting identifying details.

## 5. Analysis method

After the interviews, read the notes and assign short codes to recurring topics, such as discovery channels, information needs, review credibility and complaint difficulties. These are initial organising ideas, not predetermined findings; add or change codes when the answers require it.

Group related codes into themes. Keep an evidence trail from each theme to participant codes and questions. Include positive experiences, disagreements and exceptions. Count participants mentioning a theme rather than the number of repeated statements. Report counts with the actual sample size; do not generalise prevalence from a small convenience sample.

Compare each supported theme with the current requirements. State whether it supports an existing feature, suggests a change or raises an unresolved question. Participant suggestions do not automatically become implementation commitments. Discuss substantial scope changes with the supervisor and record feedback only when received.

## 7. Preliminary findings and design implications

These themes are based on the supplied accounts. They describe this small set of participants and do not estimate prevalence among drivers generally.

| Theme | Supporting records | Evidence and exceptions | Design implication / proposed action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal recommendations and existing relationships | P1–P3 Q2; P5 Q2; P3 Q8 | Three participants selected from personal recommendations; P5 returned to a familiar provider. P3 saw no personal need for the website. | Treat public discovery (FR-02–04) as an additional route; do not claim the website replaces personal trust for everyone. |
| Price clarity | P1–P6 Q3; P4 Q5; P5 Q3 | All six mention price or cost agreement, with different emphases. P4 reports an estimate overrun; P2 describes financial burden. | Consider clearer service descriptions (FR-02). Published estimates and price-comparison tools need a separate scope decision; do not represent them as existing requirements. |
| Specific review content | P1, P4, P5, P6 Q4 | Detailed descriptions matter, including work performed, cost and outcome. P1 and P6 distrust repetitive short comments. P2 did not consult online reviews; P3 considers them secondary. | Supports review text and visible context (FR-09, FR-13). Explain verification limits; these accounts do not prove any review was fraudulent. |
| Repair explanation and expectations | P3 Q5; P5 Q3/Q6; P6 Q6 | Prior discussion helped P3; diagnostic explanation helped P5; P6 reports missing explanation. | Consider clearer request/service descriptions (FR-02, FR-05). Diagnostic evidence sharing would require separate design and privacy decisions. |
| Mixed and negative experiences | P4 Q5/Q7; P6 Q5/Q7; P3 Q7 | P4 combined satisfaction with criticism; P6 described an unsatisfactory repair. | Retain negative and mixed reviews under FR-08–09 and FR-12–13. Completion confirmation must not mean satisfaction. |
| Timing and spare parts | P1 Q6/Q8; P4 Q2/Q6/Q8; P6 Q2/Q3 | Parts availability affected P1/P4; fast access mattered to P4/P6. | Review status communication under FR-06–07. Live booking and spare-part inventory are suggestions, not commitments for the first version. |
| Conditional usefulness | P1–P6 Q8 | Five express potential usefulness or need, including P2's brief reported answer. P5 limits usefulness to finding a new provider; P6 makes it conditional on verification. P3 sees no personal value. | Preserve disagreement and qualifications. These opinions are not evidence of adoption, usability or effectiveness. |

Maps, booking calendars, automatic estimates, parts search, warranty displays and work histories are participant suggestions. They have not been added to the implementation scope. Any change requires feasibility review and a documented decision.

## 8. Limitations and next steps

- Six individual driver interviews conducted through Threads and WhatsApp are documented through response summaries supplied by the author. The author confirms permission for anonymous academic use. Participant-level channel details, recruitment, age eligibility and duration are not recorded.
- P1 described a positive experience and willingness to recommend the service, without stating whether an online review was posted. P2 described replacement work and said the website would help them, without explicitly evaluating the repair outcome. The analysis uses these answers as supplied.
- The source material was translated and paraphrased. Ambiguous transcript fragments were not interpreted as new facts. No verbatim quotations are claimed.
- Self-reported recollections and technical explanations were not independently verified. No allegations about identifiable services are published.
- Only drivers are represented. Specialist and administrator needs still require separate evidence.
- Opinions concern a proposed concept, not observed use of the prototype or production system. Functional, usability and performance evaluation remains unexecuted.

Next, review the findings with the supervisor and record any agreed changes to the requirements. Keep any original messages and permission evidence private. Initial supervisor feedback is recorded separately; acceptance of the user-research findings has not been recorded.
